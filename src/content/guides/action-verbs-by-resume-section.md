## Why one verb for everything is weak

If every bullet starts with "Responsible for" or "Managed", the reader stops distinguishing your contributions. The fix is not to collect impressive verbs — it is to pick the verb that fits **what the section is trying to prove**.

## Summary / objective

The summary sits at the top and frames your trajectory. Verbs here should signal scope and direction, not tasks.

- Prefer: **Driving**, **Building**, **Leading**, **Specializing in**
- Avoid task-level verbs like "Handled" or "Did" — they undersell a headline section.

> *Summary: Engineer **building** data platforms that cut reporting latency for 40 analysts.*

## Work experience

This is where precise, outcome verbs belong. Match the verb to your actual role:

| Your role | Verb |
|---|---|
| You initiated the work | **Spearheaded**, **Launched**, **Piloted** |
| You led people or process | **Led**, **Directed**, **Coordinated** |
| You built or shipped | **Delivered**, **Shipped**, **Implemented** |
| You improved something | **Improved**, **Optimized**, **Streamlined** |

Keep one verb per bullet and let the metric carry the weight.

## Projects

Project sections often describe self-initiated or academic work. Verbs here can be slightly more active than experience bullets:

- Prefer: **Built**, **Designed**, **Prototyped**, **Open-sourced**
- If the project is ongoing, use present tense: *"**Building** a CLI tool used by 300 developers."*

## Skills

The Skills section is a list, not a prose section — do not force verbs here. Write the skill name and, if useful, a short proof:

> *Python — **used** to automate 20k daily report runs.*

If you do use a verb, keep it minimal and factual; "Proficient in" is acceptable but adds no evidence.

## A quick-reference table

| Section | Verb tone | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Summary | directional | Driving, Building |
| Experience | outcome-focused | Delivered, Optimized |
| Projects | active / present | Built, Prototyping |
| Skills | minimal or none | (list only) |
