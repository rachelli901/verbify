## The problem with "responsible for"

Many Chinese applicants translate 负责 directly as "responsible for". The result is a resume full of sentences like:

> *Responsible for project coordination and team communication.*

The issue isn't grammar — it's precision. "Responsible for" describes an area of accountability, but it rarely tells a recruiter what you **did**. On an English resume, every bullet should answer: *what action did you take, and what changed because of it?*

## Three levels of verb strength

| Level | Verb example | What it signals | When to use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Initiative | Spearheaded, Built, Streamlined | You started or drove it | You initiated the work |
| Management | Led, Managed, Coordinated | You led people or process | You had a team or owned a workflow |
| Collaboration | Contributed to, Liaised with, Supported | You were a contributor | You were part of a team effort |

## "负责" isn't one word

The single Chinese word 负责 maps to several different English verbs depending on context. Picking the wrong one can understate or overstate your role.

| Your actual role | Better than "responsible for" |
|---|---|
| You initiated the project | **Spearheaded** the project… |
| You led a team | **Led** a team of 6 to deliver… |
| You coordinated across teams | **Coordinated** releases across 4 teams |
| You were a contributor | **Contributed to** the redesign, which… |

## A note on accuracy

Don't pick the strongest verb just because it sounds impressive. If you were a contributor, "Contributed to" is more credible than "Led". Recruiters and interviewers will probe your claims — and an overstatement is easy to spot.

## Common mistakes

- **Overusing "Led"** — not every project you touched was led by you
- **Using "Responsible for" as a verb** — it's a state, not an action
- **Translating 参与 as "Participated in"** — "Contributed to" is stronger and more specific
