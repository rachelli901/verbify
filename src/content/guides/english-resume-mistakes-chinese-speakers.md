## 1. "Responsible for" everywhere

The literal translation of 负责 is "responsible for", so it shows up in bullet after bullet. But it describes a state of accountability, not an action.

- **Fix:** replace it with the verb that matches what you actually did — *Spearheaded*, *Led*, *Coordinated*, *Contributed to*. See [action verbs by section](/guides/action-verbs-by-resume-section) for the mapping.

## 2. Literal translation of idioms

Phrases like 吃苦耐劳 or 团结友爱 do not survive translation. "Hardworking and united" reads oddly in English and adds no evidence.

- **Fix:** drop the idiom and show the behaviour through a result. Instead of "hardworking", write a bullet with a number: *"**Delivered** 3 releases per sprint for 8 months."*

## 3. Wrong tense

A common slip is mixing present and past tense, or using present tense for a job you have left.

- **Fix:** use **past tense** for past roles (*Led*, *Built*, *Improved*). Use **present tense** only for your current role or an ongoing project (*Lead*, *Build*).

## 4. Long, padded sentences

Chinese writing sometimes favours context before the point. On an English resume, the point should come first.

- **Weak:** *In order to improve the efficiency of the team, I implemented a new workflow.*
- **Strong:** *"**Streamlined** the team workflow, cutting handoff time 30%."*

## 5. Fluff claims without proof

Words like "team player", "self-motivated", and "excellent communicator" appear on almost every resume and prove nothing on their own.

- **Fix:** either cut them, or convert them into a behaviour. "Strong communicator" becomes *"**Presented** monthly metrics to 20 stakeholders."*

## A quick self-check

Read your resume aloud. If a bullet could belong to anyone, it is too generic — add a verb and a number so it can only belong to you.
